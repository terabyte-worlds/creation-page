Overview

Shortcuts to progress are sold by the other players

The Shopping District is a multiplayer tycoon in UEFN where the shortcuts to progress are not sold by the game — they are sold by the other players in the session. Each business type produces one advantage that only its owner can sell. The currency paid lands in that owner's balance, and a market fee burns part of it out of circulation.

The full economic cycle runs today. Tested on 4 September 2026, in UEFN 42.10, in a private version with simultaneous players on separate devices: earn Tickets in the minigame zone, buy an advantage at another player's shop, watch the value land in their balance, quit Fortnite, come back, and find everything intact.

The experience

A plaza where the buildings are the progress bar

You walk into a plaza. There's a coffee shop on the corner, decorated and busy. Across from it, a restaurant that yesterday was a street stall and today has a dining room and a window display. Your friend joins the session, and the coffee kiosk he had yesterday is now a factory, standing in the exact same spot.

You head to your own plot and pick your business. The first thing you find out is that you can walk into the shop next door and buy an advantage for yourself — except it isn't paid with the currency you have. How do I get that currency? On the other side of the plaza, a different business sells a different advantage. How fast could I grow if I combined both?

The loop: you pick a business type and it starts small. You work your plot to pay for upgrades, and every upgrade physically changes what stands there — the kiosk becomes a storefront, the stall becomes a dining room. To speed up your output, you buy advantages from other players with Tickets, earned in the minigames spread around the island. Part of what you pay goes straight into that player's balance; part is burned by the market fee. That is what turns the plaza into an economy instead of a row of neighbouring plots.

Every session has a different spread of businesses, levels and advantages. If four people open coffee shops in the same session, the bonuses stack into a ceiling none of them would reach alone. If nobody opens a coffee shop, that advantage does not exist that session. Players start coordinating who opens what — behaviour that emerges from the economy, not content we wrote.

Two design decisions hold the whole thing up. First, progress is public: a business's level is readable from across the plaza, without opening a menu, because the building itself is the progress bar — and the most advanced business is where people go, because it sells the best advantages. Second, space is the interface: if the plaza hides the shops from each other, nobody finds out what the neighbour is selling and the economy never forms. Sightlines between plots, walking distance to a shop, and reading a level from the centre of the square are not decoration. They decide whether the mechanic works.

  • Plot ownership, production by manual work, and passive production scaling with level.
  • Business type selection, with the plot advancing through three levels of that type and swapping assets at every level.
  • Rules, class and HUD switching by zone.
  • Tickets earned in the minigame zone and spent in the economy.
  • Player-to-player sale of advantages, with the value landing in the shop owner's balance and a market fee taking part of the currency out of circulation.

Unique features

What makes it different

A market where the seller is another player, with a real sink

Progression shortcuts are not sold by the game. Each business type produces one advantage only its owner can sell, and the currency paid lands in that owner's balance. Every purchase also carries a market fee: of 5 units paid by the buyer, 3 reach the shop owner and 2 are burned out of circulation. Tickets cannot inflate indefinitely, and the fee is the balancing lever we tune as the economy grows. Your progress is not worth what the game says it is worth — it is worth what another player will pay for it.

Two currencies with mutually exclusive jobs

Coins are produced inside your own plot and pay only for your own upgrades. Tickets can only be earned outside it, in the minigame zones, and can only be spent on other players. A player who never leaves their plot produces a lot and trades nothing. The design makes leaving the plot the price of participating in the market.

Advantages stack, so the session sets the ceiling

Bonuses of the same type add up rather than overwrite. Four coffee shops in one session compound into a rate no single player could reach alone, and if nobody opens a coffee shop that advantage does not exist at all that session. The ceiling is not a number on your account — it is who logged in with you and what they chose to build.

Choosing a business locks a progression path

Picking a type commits the plot to that type's three levels, swapping assets at every level. Progress is legible from across the plaza without opening a single menu, because the building itself is the progress bar.

Complete solo, amplified in multiplayer

Alone, you build, produce, run the minigames and level up with nothing gated behind other people. Other players do not unlock the game — they raise its ceiling. The map is never in a broken state because the lobby happens to be empty.

Spatial design as mechanical infrastructure

I come from architecture, and here that is not a presentation detail. If the plaza hides the shops from each other, nobody discovers what the neighbour sells and the market never forms. Sightlines between plots, walking distance to a shop, and reading a business's level from the centre of the square are the design decisions that determine whether the mechanic works at all.

Built for the platform as it is, designed for what it becomes

The whole economy runs inside a single published island, which is what UEFN supports today, with the persistence layer isolated behind its own interface rather than written inline. Extending this economy across multiple islands, when the Shareable weak_map card on Epic's public roadmap ships, becomes an implementation swap rather than a rewrite. We release that module openly to other creators in Milestone 2.

Milestones

4 January to 4 July 2027 — 26 weeks

The funded window ends on a decision gate this company has already written for itself, so the plan runs to a point of evidence, not an open-ended development period.

Milestone 1 · Weeks 1–8 · 4 January to 28 February 2027

Deepening the economy

Upgrade slots inside the business: players buy items that occupy slots and raise passive production — a better espresso machine, a more elaborate counter — with the number of slots growing with the business level. Economy telemetry recording every transaction: buyer, seller, amount, fee burned. Legal entity constituted. First community publication.

Closes when a measured playtest records the complete chain for three simultaneous players — earn, buy from another player, seller credited, fee burned, progress restored after rejoining — with every transaction exported by the telemetry.

Milestone 2 · Weeks 9–17 · 1 March to 2 May 2027

The plaza

Five business types, each with its own advantage and its own stacking rules. Three minigame zones feeding the Ticket economy. The plaza rebuilt to the spatial standard the mechanic requires. Map page reworked — thumbnail, title, description — against the 0.86% click-through we measure today. Open release of the Verse persistence module.

Closes on two measured numbers: plaza-to-minigame crossing rate of at least 30%, and at least one player-to-player purchase in 60% or more of sessions with four or more players.

Milestone 3 · Weeks 18–26 · 3 May to 4 July 2027

Launch and measured operation

Public launch with a scheduled event and update calendar. Eight weeks of continuous, measured operation. Public learnings report. Tutorial series on the architecture-to-Blender-to-UEFN pipeline.

Closes on D1 of at least 25% and D7 of at least 10%, measured in a period with no paid promotion.

What other creators get

Three deliverables, budgeted and scheduled

In Milestone 1 we publish our research on the real limits of cross-island persistence in UEFN on the Epic Developer Community — sourced, dated, separating what Epic states from what the community assumes, with reproducible tests. We wrote it to decide our own architecture, and we did not find it anywhere else.

In Milestone 2 we release the persistence module openly: a wrapper over weak_map that isolates the persistence layer behind its own interface, so a map does not need a rewrite the day the Shareable weak_map card on Epic's public Fortnite developer roadmap ships. Our own economy runs inside a single published island, which is what UEFN supports today, and it is built this way for the same reason.

In Milestone 3 we publish a learnings report from eight weeks of live operation — retention, crossing rates, and what the market fee did to the economy — plus a tutorial series on the pipeline from architectural modelling to a shipped UEFN island.

Why Terabyte Worlds

Fortnite's UGC is full of activities and empty of places

I am an architect, and one question runs through that entire profession: what makes someone want to come back to a place? In a plaza, an arcade, a shopping street, the answer was never the decoration — it was what happens there between people. In UEFN I found a way to ask that same question at a reach built architecture would never give me.

We have published two maps: a Red vs Blue and Multiverse Gun Game, the latter selected among the strongest projects by the instructors of the UEFN Student Bootcamp 2026. The systems behind both were written in Verse from scratch, learning the language over the course of production.

Those two maps produced the data that started this project. Epic's own Discover delivered 28,144 impressions to Multiverse Gun Game, which converted to 242 clicks, 82 plays, and a D1 of 0.41%. The platform gave us an audience for free and the audience did not come back — not because the map was bad, but because nothing inside it depended on anyone else. Every player raised their own number and left.

That is the problem this company decided to attack, and it has a name: Fortnite's UGC is full of activities and empty of places. The Shopping District is our first serious attempt at building a place, and the first one we can prove with numbers.
